Beef-flavoured fries: McDonald's 10-million-dollar apology to Hindus and vegetarians

In 2001, a Seattle lawyer sued McDonald's: its fries, cooked in vegetable oil since 1990, contained a flavouring of beef origin. The case ended with a public apology and a 10-million-dollar settlement, whose distribution remained contested until 2005.

This article is a background document. It reports public facts and court decisions, without taking sides. The sources are cited at the end of the article; reported statements are attributed to their authors.

In July 1990, McDonald's announced that it would cook its fries in vegetable oil in the United States and abandon the beef tallow used until then. According to the UPI agency, the change, presented as a step towards healthier eating, was to be completed before the end of the year. Eleven years later, this choice was at the heart of several class actions: those who brought them accused the brand of having let people believe that its fries were suitable for vegetarians, whereas a flavouring of beef origin was added to them.

A vegetable oil, a beef flavouring

Since 1990, McDonald's had stated that its fries were cooked in pure vegetable oil, which is the case in the restaurants. But they are first par-fried at the suppliers, then frozen, and it is at this stage that a flavouring is added. In May 2001, the company confirmed to the Seattle Times that it was a “minuscule” amount of beef extract.

The ingredient list published by the brand at the time mentioned a “natural flavour”, without specifying its origin. According to the newspaper, one had to ask customer service to find out. McDonald's spokesman, Walt Riker, maintained that using this term to designate a beef extract complied with the guidelines of the Food and Drug Administration, the federal agency responsible for food.

The Seattle lawsuit and its repercussions

On 1 May 2001, the lawyer Harish Bharti filed suit in the King County Superior Court, in Seattle, on behalf of three plaintiffs: two vegetarian Hindus and one non-Hindu vegetarian. His complaint relied on an email from the company mentioning small amounts of beef flavouring and sought unquantified damages for “emotional distress”. According to the lawyer, a reasonable person, told that the fries are prepared in “100% vegetable” oil, would believe them suitable for vegetarians.

McDonald's replied that it had never presented its fries as vegetarian and that it provides their composition to anyone who asks. It also disputed one point of the complaint, which referred to tallow: in its view, it was a beef extract. In the course of May, it published on its website an apology for the “confusion” caused, while denying that it had added the flavouring “in secret”.

Other actions were brought in 2001 in California, Illinois and New Jersey, as well as in Texas according to the press. In India, in May 2001, activists from the Bharatiya Janata Party threw cow dung at a restaurant in south Bombay, UPI reported, and a crowd attacked an outlet on the outskirts of the city, according to the Indian daily The Tribune. McDonald's India stated that the fries served in the country do not contain beef.

A 10-million-dollar settlement

An agreement dated 22 March 2002 brought together the settlement of the proceedings before the Circuit Court of Cook County, Illinois, where Judge Richard Siebel sat. McDonald's undertook to pay 10 million dollars into a fund for non-profit organisations: 60% for vegetarian organisations, 20% for Hindu or Sikh organisations, 10% for children's nutrition and the fight against child hunger, and 10% for knowledge of kosher dietary rules.

The agreement also provided for a public apology, published on the brand's website and in specialist publications, the creation of an advisory board, better information on the origin of ingredients, and instructions to managers on how to answer customers' questions. Each named plaintiff could receive up to 4,000 dollars, and lawyers' fees were capped at 2.452 million dollars. In it, McDonald's “expressly denies any liability”.

In June 2002, the brand published its apology.

“McDonald's sincerely apologises to Hindus, vegetarians and others for failing to provide them with the kind of information they needed to make informed dietary choices in our US restaurants”McDonald's, June 2002 - translated from English

The text acknowledged that, when the switch to vegetable oil was made in the early 1990s, “mistakes were made” in communicating about the ingredients, and that fries and hash browns sold in its US restaurants had been wrongly described as “vegetarian”. After a preliminary approval, the court approved the amount of the settlement at the end of October 2002, without yet validating the list of beneficiaries.

A distribution contested until 2005

The choice of beneficiary organisations, proposed by McDonald's and some of the plaintiffs' lawyers, drew objections. In January 2003, Harish Bharti, who did not oppose the settlement itself, asked the judge to entrust the selection to an independent third party. Vegetarian and animal protection groups also contested the list, according to the Chicago Sun-Times. McDonald's lawyer, Peter Hecker, for his part described all the organisations selected by the company as “responsible”.

In spring 2003, the judge excluded three of the 26 proposed organisations because of conflicts of interest involving lawyers in the case. On 19 May 2003, he settled a list of 24 beneficiaries. The largest, the Vegetarian Resource Group, received 1.4 million dollars. Appeals were lodged by vegetarians and, separately, by a Muslim organisation. They were dismissed in 2005, and the payments were made in July 2005, according to Hinduism Today, itself a beneficiary of the fund.

What the label says today

In the United States, the ingredient list for the fries now mentions a “natural beef flavour” (Natural Beef Flavor), which contains, among other things, hydrolysed milk according to the US press. Several media outlets conclude that these fries are suitable for neither vegans nor vegetarians.

The 2002 agreement covered the products served in all of the brand's US restaurants, including those run by franchisees, whereas the flavouring at issue was added upstream, at the suppliers. The case illustrates the role of the head of the network in the information given to customers about the composition of the products sold under its brand.
