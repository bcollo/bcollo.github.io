A 10-cent toy: how McDonald's got around San Francisco's anti-Happy Meal ordinance

In 2010, Santa Clara and then San Francisco banned giving away a toy with a children's meal that is too high in calories, despite the veto of Mayor Gavin Newsom. On the day the rule came into force, the city's McDonald's restaurants started selling the toy for 10 cents.

This article is a background document. It reports public facts and court decisions, without taking sides. The sources are cited at the end of the article; reported statements are attributed to their authors.

On 1 December 2011, in San Francisco, a new law comes into force, it bans giving away a toy with a children's meal that does not meet certain nutritional criteria. That same day, the McDonald's restaurants in the city continues to give a toy with the Happy Meal, but now it's price is 10 cents. The story of this workaround begins a year and a half earlier, in a neighbouring county.

Santa Clara leads the way

First of all, on 27 April 2010, Santa Clara Countys supervisors, in Silicon Valley, votes an ordinance that bans restaurants from giving away a toy with a meal that is too high in calories. According to the radio programme Marketplace, the text is adopted by 3 votes to 2. The thresholds are 485 calories and 600 milligrams of sodium, with limits on fats and added sugars. A few years earlier, the chain had already been taken to court in New York over the issue of childhood obesity.

The text was mostly symbolic. According to the Associated Press news agency, it only covered the countys unincorporated areas, that is too say about a dozen fast-food outlets and a few family restaurants. The fines planned was from 250 dollars for a first offence up to 1,000 dollars for the following ones. A McDonald's spokesperson said they were disappointed, while pointing out that the ordinance did not affect any of it's restaurants in the county.

Supervisor Ken Yeager, who were behind the text, defended his ideas like this:

“This ordinance is not about toys. Obviously, toys in themselves do not make children obese.”Ken Yeager, Santa Clara County supervisor, 27 April 2010 - translated from English

With this sentence, he wanted to show that the problem wasnt the toy it self but the fact of using it to draw children towards fatty and sugary meals.

San Francisco overrides the mayor's veto

Next, it is San Francisco that takes up the idea, with the Healthy Food Incentives Ordinance (No. 290-10), sponsored by Supervisor Eric Mar. The criteria are stricter: fewer than 600 calories per meal, no more than 35% of calories from fat, less than 640 milligrams of sodium, and above all at least half a cup of fruit and three quarters of a cup of vegetables, according to the summary published by the CDC, the federal public health agency.

On Tuesday 9 November 2010, the Board of Supervisors adopts the text by 8 votes to 3. Mayor Gavin Newsom vetoes it on 12 November. According to CNN, he said: “It is parents, not politicians, who should decide what their children eat, especially when it comes to spending their own money.” He also spoke of an “unprecedented and reckless government intrusion” into families' choices.

But the majority of 8 votes out of 11 was enough to override it. On 23 November 2010, the supervisors confirm their vote and the veto falls. San Francisco thus becomes the first major American city too adopt this type of rule, with the date of entry into force set at 1 December 2011.

The 10-cent workaround

Then, on the eve of the entry into force, the press announces the solution chosen by McDonald's. According to CBS News and the Associated Press, the city's restaurants will charge 10 cents for the toy to customers who asks for it with their Happy Meal. Scott Rodrick, a franchisee who owns 10 of the 19 McDonald's restaurants in San Francisco, states that this practice respects “the letter of the law”. The money raised is to go towards building a new Ronald McDonald House for the families of sick children.

According to McDonald's USA's head of media relations, the price was set after customers was asked, and they reportedly felt that 10 cents was “fair and reasonable”. For his part, Eric Mar spoke of a “marketing ploy”, but he said he didnt intend to amend his ordinance because of it.

In 2014, a study published by the CDC analysed what happened. The two big chains studied has adopted the same strategy, the toy sold for 10 cents on it's own, and 88% of customers who bought a children's meal also took the toy. No children's meal from these two chains met the ordinance's criteria during the whole study. The researchers explains that the text only banned giving away a “free” toy, which left the possibility of selling it. In Santa Clara, whose ordinance did not contain the word “free”, the restaurants had took a different approach.

The Parham v. McDonald's complaint

At the same time, the fight also moved too the courts. On 15 December 2010, the Center for Science in the Public Interest (CSPI) filed a class action before the Superior Court of California in San Francisco, the case Monet Parham et al v. McDonald's Corp, No. 10-506178. According to Reuters, Monet Parham was a mother of two daughters, a health educator for the State of California, who lived in Sacramento. The complaint argue that using toys to attract children was an unfair business practice under California consumer law, and it didnt claim any damages. “This lawsuit is about change, not about money,” said Stephen Gardner, the CSPIs director of litigation. McDonald's had replied through its spokeswoman that it intended to defend its brand and its products “vigorously”.

To conclude, in early April 2012, Judge Richard Kramer dismissed the complaint. According to Fox News, the dismissal was made “with prejudice”, which prevented the plaintiffs from filing an amended complaint, and according to ABC News the judge gave no reason. The San Francisco and Santa Clara ordinances were not affected by this judgment.

This case shows something fairly simple: faced with a local rule, the response come from the citys restaurants, most of them was held by one franchisee, whereas menu changes were decided at the level of the chain. The Happy Meal, for its part, kept its toy.
