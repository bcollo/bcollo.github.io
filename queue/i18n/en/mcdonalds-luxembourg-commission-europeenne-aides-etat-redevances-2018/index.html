Taxation · 18 November 2026

McDonald's paid no tax on its European royalties, and Brussels ruled that this was legal

From 2015 to 2018, the European Commission investigated two tax rulings granted in 2009 by Luxembourg to McDonald's Europe Franchising. It concluded that there was no State aid, while acknowledging that the profits concerned had not been taxed anywhere.

This article is a background document. It reports public facts and court decisions, without taking sides. The sources are cited at the end of the article; reported statements are attributed to their authors.

On 3 December 2015, the European Commission announces the opening of a formal investigation into the tax treatment granted by Luxembourg to McDonald's. The subsidiary in question is called McDonald's Europe Franchising, it colects the royalties that franchised restaurants in Europe and Russia pay to use the brand. According to the Commission, this company has paid no corporate income tax in Luxembourg since 2009, even though it recorded profits of more than 250 million € in 2013.

A trade union report at the origin of the case

First of all, we have to go back a few month earlier. On 25 February 2015, a coalition of European and American trade unions, together with the British charity War on Want, publishes a report on the group's taxes. According to this document, handed that same morning too several Members of the European Parliament according to La Libre Belgique, McDonald's allegedly avoided paying more than one billion euros in taxes in Europe between 2009 and 2013. The authors claims that the Luxembourg structure collected more than 3.7 billion euros in royalties for only 16 million euros in tax.

The report, quoted by EUobserver, goes further and considers it “likely” that the company concluded a secret tax deal with Luxembourg. It also points out that in July 2009 the companys European head office had been moved from London to Geneva. For its part, McDonald's Belgium said it was “enormously surprised and affected by these allegations”. As for the Commission, it later explained that it had already asked for information on these rulings in the summer of 2014, after press articles, and that trade unions then sent it further information. According to AFP, the case follows in the wake of the LuxLeaks revelations about the secret tax deals made by big companys with the Grand Duchy.

Two rulings signed six months apart

At the heart of the case are two tax rulings, which Brussels calls “rulings”. The first one is granted in March 2009. It confirms that McDonald's Europe Franchising does not have too pay corporate income tax in Luxembourg, because its profits will be taxed in the United States, under the tax treaty between the two countries. In exchange, the company is suppose to prove every year that the royalties transferred to the United States via Switzerland are actually declared and taxed there.

Then, things gets complicated. According to the Commission, the profits were in reality not taxable in the United States. McDonald's argued that its US branch was a “permanent establishment” under Luxembourg law, but not under US law. In September 2009, a second ruling therefore exempts the company from proving that it's income is taxed across the Atlantic. The result, according to the preliminary analysis of 2015: these profits are taxed neither on one side nor on the other.

“Double taxation treaties between two countries are meant to avoid cases of double taxation, not to justify double non-taxation.”Margrethe Vestager, European Commissioner for Competition, 3 December 2015

That very same day, Luxembourg let it be known, according to Paperjam, that it considers that “no particular tax treatment or selective advantage was granted to McDonald's”. On the companys side, it says it complies with all the laws in Europe and that it paid 2.1 billion dollars in corporate income tax between 2010 and 2014.

The decision of 19 September 2018

Nearly three years later, on 19 September 2018, the Commission delivers its decision in case SA.38945. It come to the conclusion that the non-taxation of these profits did not amount to illegal State aid. For Brussels, the US branch did not meet the conditions of US law to be a permanent establishment, but its activities did meet those of Luxembourg law. Luxembourg could therefore exempt this income without breaching the tax treaty, and it didnt do any special favour too McDonald's.

Margrethe Vestager explains that the double non-taxation results “from a mismatch between Luxembourg and US tax laws, and not from special treatment by Luxembourg”. But she immediately adds:

“Of course, the fact remains that McDonald's did not pay any taxes on the profits concerned – which is not in line with the principle of tax fairness.”Margrethe Vestager, European Commissioner for Competition, 19 September 2018

Unlike Fiat, Starbucks or Apple, which were the subject of negative decisions in similar cases, McDonald's therefore had nothing too pay back. According to AFP, a spokesperson for the group said the groups companies had paid more than 3 billion dollars in corporate income tax in the Union between 2013 and 2017. War on Want, a complainant in the case, said for its part that the Commission had “missed an opportunity”.

A loophole that Luxembourg says it wants to close

To conclude, the case above all brought to light a mismatch between two tax systems. The Commission welcomed the fact that the Luxembourg government had presented, on 19 June 2018, a draft law aligning the provision in question with the OECD's work against base erosion and profit shifting. In France, the group also concluded a judicial public interest agreement in 2022, another case dealt with separately.

This case also shows how much the franchise model relies on royalties: its the money paid by the restaurants to the franchisor, for the brand and the services, that was at the centre of the investigation, and the way the networks head organises the flow of these sums upwards can become a question of European law.
